Specialization & Generalization

A practical guide to superclasses, subclasses, inheritance, constraints, relational mapping and viva answers.

Important: CampusOS currently has separate student and faculty tables and role-based portal accounts. It does not implement a Person superclass or an EER ISA hierarchy. The Person examples in this document are proposed designs used to explain the concepts, not features already implemented.

1. Specialization: top-down

Specialization divides a broad entity type into more specific subtypes based on distinguishing properties or responsibilities. The broad type is the superclass/supertype; the specific types are subclasses/subtypes.

Person
 ├── Student  (enrollment number, admission year)
 └── Faculty  (employee code, designation)

A Student is a Person, and a Faculty member is a Person. Shared attributes such as name, email and date of birth belong to Person; subtype-specific attributes belong to the subtype.

Why use it?

  • Represent meaningful categories while storing common facts in a consistent place.
  • Apply shared relationships or constraints to the superclass.
  • Keep subtype-specific properties out of unrelated records.

Viva: “Specialization is top-down: start with Person, then identify Student and Faculty as more specific entity types. A subtype inherits its identity and common attributes from the supertype.”

Not every category needs specialization. A status column such as Active/Inactive describes state, not necessarily a different entity subtype.

2. Generalization: bottom-up

Generalization starts with multiple entity types and recognizes a shared higher-level abstraction. Starting with Student and Faculty, we notice shared names/contact information and propose Person as their superclass.

ConceptDirectionQuestion asked
SpecializationGeneral → specificWhich distinct subtypes of Person are needed?
GeneralizationSpecific → generalWhich common entity can unify Student and Faculty?

The final hierarchy can look identical; the distinction is the design reasoning used to reach it.

Inheritance and ISA

Subtypes inherit the supertype identity, attributes and applicable relationship participation. A subtype row represents the same real-world entity, not an independent copy. In a shared-primary-key relational design, student.person_id is both its PK and an FK to person.person_id.

Specialization versus aggregation and ordinary relationships

  • ISA: Student is a Person — subtype membership.
  • Association: Student enrolls in Offering — two different entities linked through enrollment, not inheritance.
  • Part-of: a component belongs to a whole; it is not automatically an ISA relation.
  • EER aggregation: treating a relationship and its participating entities as a higher-level unit for another relationship.
  • SQL aggregation: COUNT, SUM and GROUP BY summarize rows; this is a different meaning of “aggregation.”

Viva: “Generalization is bottom-up. It extracts common features from existing entity types into a supertype. It is not a SQL GROUP BY operation and it is not the same as a foreign-key relationship.”

3. The two independent constraints

A. Disjoint versus overlapping

Disjoint: one superclass entity may belong to at most one subtype in this specialization. For example, an account with exactly one stored admin/student role behaves as an exclusive role choice, but that alone does not create subtype tables.

Overlapping: a person may belong to multiple subtypes, such as a postgraduate student who is also employed as faculty. The institution’s business rules determine whether this is permitted.

B. Total versus partial completeness

Total: every superclass entity must belong to at least one subtype. For example, if Person exists only for Student or Faculty records, no unrelated Person rows should exist.

Partial: a superclass entity can exist without belonging to the modeled subtypes, such as a visitor or external collaborator in a broader Person registry.

CombinationMeaning
Total + disjointEvery Person is exactly one modeled subtype.
Total + overlappingEvery Person is at least one subtype and may be several.
Partial + disjointA Person may be none or one subtype.
Partial + overlappingA Person may be none, one or several subtypes.

How membership is determined

Predicate/attribute-defined: membership follows a condition or discriminator value. User-defined: membership is explicitly assigned according to application rules. For overlapping categories, a single exclusive discriminator may be insufficient.

Typical EER notation: ISA connectors or a specialization circle; d for disjoint and o for overlapping; double line for total participation, single for partial. Notation conventions vary by textbook. No diagram is required to explain these definitions.

4. Mapping a hierarchy into SQL tables

StrategyBenefitsTrade-offs
Superclass + subtype tablesCommon facts stored once; subtype fields isolated; shared identity explicit.Joins needed. Disjointness/completeness may need extra constraints or application logic.
Single table + discriminatorSimple shared queries; one identity table; no join for common/subtype attributes.Many nullable subtype columns; conditional validation; overlapping categories are less natural.
Concrete subtype tables onlySimple independent subtype access, no superclass join.Common attributes repeat in schema; cross-type identity and overlapping membership are harder to maintain.

Proposed shared-primary-key example (not current CampusOS schema)

CREATE TABLE person (
  person_id INTEGER PRIMARY KEY,
  full_name TEXT NOT NULL,
  email TEXT NOT NULL UNIQUE
);
CREATE TABLE student_person (
  person_id INTEGER PRIMARY KEY
    REFERENCES person(person_id),
  enrollment_number TEXT NOT NULL UNIQUE
);
CREATE TABLE faculty_person (
  person_id INTEGER PRIMARY KEY
    REFERENCES person(person_id),
  employee_code TEXT NOT NULL UNIQUE
);

A subtype row must reference an existing Person. Its PK prevents duplicate membership within that subtype. The same person_id can appear in both subtype tables, so this base structure permits overlap. A Person with no subtype is allowed, so it is partial unless further rules are added.

A foreign key alone does not enforce total specialization or disjointness. Enforcing those across tables requires deliberate transaction design, appropriate triggers/constraints or a schema strategy that makes invalid combinations impossible. SQLite does not offer PostgreSQL-style deferred constraint triggers.

5. What CampusOS actually implements

Actual tablePrimary keyMeaning
studentstudent_idIndependent student entity with its own identity, contact and academic attributes.
facultyfaculty_idIndependent faculty entity with employee, contact and department attributes.
portal_accountaccount_idAuthentication identity, role, optional linked student_id and password hash/salt.
portal_sessiontoken_hashExpiring login session referencing account_id.

There is no person table. Matching student_id and faculty_id numeric values do not mean the same person. Similar attribute names do not prove inheritance. Independent records may happen to represent the same real-world person, but the schema has no shared identity linking them.

Account role rules

  • role is restricted to admin or student.
  • An admin account must have student_id NULL.
  • A student account must have a non-NULL student_id referencing student.
  • student_id is unique in portal_account, preventing multiple linked accounts for the same student under this design.
  • The UI and server enforce role-specific access. This is RBAC-like authorization with a single role field, not Person–Student–Faculty subtype inheritance.

If asked why specialization was not implemented

“Our requirements can be served by independent student and faculty entities, plus a linked authentication account. We did not need a unified person registry or model dual student/faculty membership. A Person supertype would be a reasonable future extension, but I would not claim it exists in our current implementation.”

Specialization is an EER modeling technique, not a mandatory normalization stage. Separate student/faculty tables do not automatically violate 3NF. Normalization concerns functional dependencies within relations; choose inheritance based on domain identity and requirements.

6. Future extension and viva answers

How to introduce Person safely

  1. Decide identity matching rules; do not merge people by name alone.
  2. Choose overlap: can one person be both student and faculty? Choose total/partial completeness based on requirements.
  3. Create Person and assign stable person IDs. Migrate verified common facts; resolve conflicting email/contact data.
  4. Link student/faculty to Person while preserving existing IDs or deliberately migrate all references in a transaction-controlled process.
  5. Adapt APIs, joins, forms and accounts. Decide whether accounts link to Person or a particular role identity.
  6. Test that enrollments, grades, attendance, assignments and portal authorization remain unchanged.

30-second summary: “Specialization starts with a general entity and creates specific subtypes; generalization works in the opposite direction. Subtypes inherit common identity and attributes. Disjointness controls whether multiple membership is allowed; completeness controls whether every supertype must have a subtype. CampusOS currently has independent Student and Faculty tables and role-based accounts, not a Person ISA hierarchy.”
